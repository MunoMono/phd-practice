# Testamentary traces of contested design knowledge: mobilising the Royal College of Art's Department of Design Research archive

**Audience:** readers, researchers, archive staff and project collaborators
**Status:** report
**Version:** 1.0
**Date:** 14 August 2026

## What this guide is for

This guide explains what each screen in the Turin research instrument is for in everyday language. It is a guide to working with the archive, not a guide to making historical claims. The application helps people find material, compare it, keep track of gaps and record how a conclusion was reached. It does not turn a search result or a generated answer into a fact by itself.

## Research scope

The Turin experiment concerns the Royal College of Art Department of Design Research (DDR), 1965–1985.

The pages work as a connected route through the material. You can begin with the **Workbench** for an overview, inspect the **Sources**, ask a carefully bounded question through **Source interrogation**, and then use the other pages to review gaps, connections, patterns and claims.

## Moving around

The header provides the main sections: **Workbench**, **Sources**, **Analysis**, **Evidence** and **Documentation**. The secondary navigation changes to show the pages inside the section you are using. On a small screen, the navigation opens from the menu button.

The light and dark appearance button changes the reading theme only; it does not change the archive or the research record. The account button signs you in or out. Signing in protects the working research material and makes the same pages available to authorised collaborators.

Older web addresses still lead to the current pages. For example, older addresses for the corpus, asking questions, missingness, claims, visual analytics, audit and experiments take you to their current counterparts. They do not open separate or duplicate tools.

## Workbench

The **Workbench** is the front door to the instrument. It shows whether the local research model is available, how much of the frozen research corpus is searchable and how the curated corpus differs from the wider archive inventory. This helps you see the conditions of the work before you begin.

Use the links and panels here to move into a task. The workbench is not a place to make a finding. It is a briefing screen: it tells you what is ready, what is in scope and where to go next.

## Sources

The **Sources** page is where you inspect the available archival material before asking the system to work with it. You can search by a word, document identifier, year or processing state. Selecting an item opens its details, including its title, archive links, source file information and preparation status.

This page is useful when you want to check that a document is the right one, see what record it belongs to or understand what material is available before forming a question. It keeps archive description, policy decisions and the wording of the source itself distinct, so a catalogue label is not mistaken for evidence from the document.

## Source interrogation

The **Source interrogation** page turns a question into a visible trail through the archive. Enter a research question and run the interrogation. The page returns a provisional response together with the passages, document details, page references and archive identifiers that were used.

Read the response alongside the source stack, not instead of it. The page lets you inspect the route from question, through the local Granite model, to retrieved passages and their archive records. It can also preserve and export that trail for later review. A response is a starting point for interpretation, not a final historical answer.

## Absences

The **Absences** page records gaps that matter to the research. A gap may be a failed search, a thin catalogue description, an unavailable document, an access restriction or a person who is rarely represented. The page lets you filter these entries, add review notes, set their status and export the resulting list.

Its central purpose is restraint. A missing result does not prove that something never happened. Instead, the page helps you say what the present material, description or search process cannot support, and keeps that limitation available for further investigation.

## Cross-readings

The **Cross-readings** page is for putting oral testimony and archival records into conversation. You can add or select a passage of testimony, use it to find related documents, and record whether a document supports, complicates, contradicts or has no documentary trace of that passage.

The page does not force recollection and documentation to agree. It gives each its own place and makes the relationship between them visible. You can add notes, review the proposed links and export a map of the comparison for discussion or further research.

## Semantic atlas

The **Semantic atlas** offers a visual way to explore how parts of the collection sit near one another. It groups documents or passages by similarities in their language and lets you filter the view, select points, examine clusters and read the source details behind a visual pattern.

Use it to generate questions, not to treat a visual grouping as proof. A nearby point may suggest a comparison worth reading; it does not establish a relationship on its own. The page can export a short record of the view or a cluster note so that an exploratory observation is not lost.

## Claims and evidence

The **Claims and evidence** page is a disciplined place to review proposed statements. It lists claims, shows the material linked to them, records their current level of support and allows a reviewer to add notes or change the review status.

It is designed to make qualifications visible. A claim can be supported, partly supported, unresolved or otherwise limited. The page can export the claim list, but it does not present unsupported material as settled. Use it when moving from an interesting observation toward language that could responsibly appear in research writing.

## Research runs

The **Research runs** page stores individual, bounded Granite experiments. Each saved run keeps the original question, the corpus used, the retrieved passages, which passages fitted into the prompt, the model settings, the generated response and the provenance check. Failed runs remain visible too, because they are useful evidence about the conditions of the instrument.

You can select a run to read its source evidence, generated analysis, scoped missingness note and reproducibility details. The research evidence itself is preserved. A separate researcher assessment area lets a person score relevance, provenance and restraint, add notes and classify problems without rewriting the original run. JSON and Markdown exports make the record portable.

## Provenance

The **Provenance** page is a review desk and export hub. It brings together recent source interrogations, recorded absences, testimony-to-record mappings, claims, archive authorities and model readiness information. It also provides download actions for the underlying records.

This page gathers the history around research activity; it does not create a new finding. It is the right place to check what exists across the instrument, prepare material for review and see how separate pieces of work connect.

## Documentation

The **Documentation** page is a read-only library for the project’s longer texts. It currently includes the expanded abstract, the five research instruments, the Turin Technology Statement of Work and this guide. Documents are shown newest first, with their date, status and source format visible in the catalogue.

Markdown documents are presented with Carbon-styled headings, lists, quotations, code blocks and scrollable tables. The **Open source document** action opens the canonical published document. Reading a document here does not change it; the page is for shared context and durable project memory.

## A careful working sequence

For a typical piece of work, begin by checking the **Workbench**, then inspect relevant material in **Sources**. Use **Source interrogation** to form a source-backed trail. If the material is thin or a search does not return what you expected, record that condition in **Absences** rather than treating it as proof of historical absence.

Use **Cross-readings** and the **Semantic atlas** to open comparisons and questions. Bring a proposed statement to **Claims and evidence** for review. When a bounded Granite experiment is appropriate, preserve it in **Research runs**. Finally, use **Provenance** to gather the records and exports needed for discussion, writing or audit.

Throughout the instrument, keep three things separate: what a source document says, what archive description says about that document, and what a researcher or model infers from the available material. That separation is what keeps the work inspectable.
