# Archival activation and synthetic heritage: generative AI, contested design knowledge and the RCA Department of Design Research archive

## Abstract

Generative AI can make cultural-heritage records easier to find and discuss, but it can also produce a smooth account of a past that the surviving material does not support. This paper reports an early practice-led experiment using a bounded retrieval-and-inference system with records from the Royal College of Art's Department of Design Research (DDR). It asks whether such a system can make dispersed traces of design knowledge more legible without presenting generated text as recovered historical fact. Twelve questions were tested across known relationships, contested interpretations and scoped missingness. In every case, the system was designed to retain the difference between what a document says, what archival metadata records, what later testimony recalls, and what the system can only infer. The principal finding is not that AI can complete an incomplete archive. It is that a carefully designed system can make the route from question to source, interpretation and limit easier to inspect. The paper proposes *scoped missingness* as a practical principle for synthetic heritage: a clear statement of what a defined digitised corpus cannot establish.

## 1. Introduction

Cultural-heritage institutions have long used new media to preserve, describe and share the past. Generative AI is the latest and perhaps most difficult development in that history. It can help people search large collections, bring distant records into conversation and produce readable accounts from scattered material. At the same time, it can make uncertain interpretations sound settled. A well-written answer can conceal the fact that a source was partial, that a catalogue record was mistaken for direct evidence, or that a later recollection was treated as a contemporary account.

These concerns are central to current discussion of synthetic heritage: the use of generative systems to combine surviving historical elements with newly produced material. The question is not simply whether an answer is plausible. It is whether people can see where it came from, what kind of evidence it uses, and where the record stops. This matters especially for archives that are incomplete, unevenly digitised and organised through institutional categories which are themselves part of their history.

This paper examines an early experiment developed within a practice-led PhD on the Royal College of Art's Department of Design Research. The Department was a significant site for post-war design research, teaching, computing and design education, but its surviving record is dispersed across reports, course materials, project files, archive descriptions and later oral histories. The experiment asks: can retrieval-augmented inference make these testamentary traces more legible without treating generated synthesis as historical recovery?

The answer offered here is deliberately modest. The experiment does not show that a language model can reconstruct the history of the DDR. It shows how a bounded system can support inspectable relationships between records. Its most important outcome is a method for slowing down overconfident interpretation: preserve provenance, distinguish source types, keep time visible, mark synthesis, and state what the available corpus does not establish.

The case is necessarily situated. The DDR was part of a particular British art-and-design institution, and its records bear the priorities, vocabularies and survival patterns of that setting. The argument should not be taken as a universal account of how heritage collections ought to be computationally activated. It is a practical examination of what becomes possible, and what becomes risky, when generative systems work with an archive whose institutional memory is unevenly available.

## 2. The DDR archive and testamentary traces

The DDR archive is not a single, complete account of a department. It is a set of surviving traces made at different times and for different purposes. A Rector's report may record an institutional decision or planned change. A course document may describe teaching offered at a particular moment. A project report may identify work undertaken and people credited in a specific context. An archive catalogue can connect records through controlled terms. An oral-history interview may offer a valuable later recollection, while remaining a recollection shaped by time, perspective and the interview situation.

This distinction is important because historical questions commonly cross those categories. A question about a person's role might require a staff list, a project acknowledgement and an employment record. A question about the Design Education Unit might draw on a prospectus, a programme document and a later interview. A question about closure may find a statement that closure was planned, but not the minutes, policy rationale or decision-maker needed to explain why.

I use *testamentary traces* to describe these partial materials. They are not fragments waiting to be assembled into a single final story. They are remains through which past work, disagreement, institutional purpose and absence can become perceptible. Their force lies partly in their incompleteness. To treat every connection between them as proof is to replace the archive's unevenness with an account that is easier to read but harder to justify.

For the DDR, this is more than a technical issue. The Department's history includes design methods, research practice, education and computing, all fields where later accounts can readily turn a dispersed set of activities into a coherent origin story. The archive offers evidence of these activities, but it does not always offer the full sequence of decisions, responsibilities or responses needed to make stronger claims. The challenge is therefore twofold: make records more findable, and make the limits around them more visible.

Institutional organisation also shapes what can be found. A catalogue term may bring two names together because an archivist has assigned both to a parent record; that is meaningful description, but it is not the same thing as a jointly authored document. An employment authority can give dates and a job title without explaining day-to-day duties. A project acknowledgement may show that a person typed a report, but it cannot by itself establish their intellectual contribution. These are not defects in individual records. They are reminders that a record's form controls the kind of question it can answer.

The language of absence needs equal care. A missing decision minute in a digitised corpus does not demonstrate that no decision minute was ever made. Nor does a lack of evidence about teachers' reception show that a programme had no effect. It only permits a narrower, more defensible statement: the accessible material currently examined does not establish the claim. This distinction protects both historical enquiry and the archive. It keeps a present condition of access from being mistaken for a settled fact about the past.

## 3. Synthetic heritage and archival legibility

Generative systems are often assessed through fluency. In cultural heritage, fluency is not enough. A convincing answer may turn a shared keyword into collaboration, a project lead into evidence of individual action, or a retrospective statement into a contemporary fact. Such mistakes are not merely factual errors. They change how authority is distributed between archive, institution, witness, researcher and system.

The useful question is therefore one of archival legibility. By this I mean the practical ability to encounter a record, understand its source and date, follow its relation to other records, and recognise what it cannot establish. Retrieval can improve this legibility by bringing relevant passages into view. Generative synthesis can reduce it when it merges those passages too quickly. The issue is not whether generation should be prohibited. It is whether generated language remains answerable to the evidence that made it possible.

This shifts attention away from the model alone. A responsible system depends on the full evidential arrangement: the corpus boundary, the retrieval route, the types of material held apart, the record of exact passages, the distinction between metadata and documentary text, and the point at which a researcher checks the result. In this sense, synthetic heritage is not simply generated content. It is the design of conditions under which generated content may be read critically.

The central problem is therefore not whether a model can produce a plausible account. It is whether the path from source to generated claim remains visible enough to be questioned. The experiment treats the model as one participant in that arrangement, not as a historical authority. It is allowed to help organise and express material, but it is not allowed to turn proximity into proof or silence into an explanation.

The distinction is particularly important for textual rather than visual forms of synthetic heritage. This project does not create reconstructed images, simulated environments or substitute heritage objects. Its synthetic element is generated prose: a proposed relation among records, expressed in ordinary language. That language can still produce an apparent historical world. A sentence that says someone initiated a programme, defined a field or caused an institutional change can travel well beyond the interface that produced it. For this reason, textual synthesis needs its own safeguards against false completion.

## 4. Research design

The experiment uses a digitised DDR evidence surface containing 27,997 PID-backed text chunks, associated archival metadata and text passages extracted from available material. Chunks without a PID-backed document record were excluded from the experimental vector scope. It was tested in a live research interface whose UAT captures are retained in the `uat_screens` folders. These captures evidence the operational workflow; the findings matrix records the analytical assessment.

The system follows an archive-first approach. It retrieves text passages and source metadata before any final synthesis is shown. Each passage remains linked to stable document, chunk and, where available, PID information. The system treats several forms of material differently: direct documentary text; archive or authority metadata; contemporary institutional records; and later oral testimony. This is not a hierarchy in which one source automatically cancels another. It is a way of preventing unlike forms of evidence from being silently merged.

Questions are routed according to their evidential shape. Named-person and project questions seek direct passages alongside relevant authority context. Concept questions reserve more than one source family so that a shared term does not become a false consensus. Temporal questions distinguish contemporary records from retrospective accounts. Questions about absence use a scoped-missingness route: they seek the nearest relevant traces and state what those traces do not establish.

Qwen was used as a bounded local inference layer. Where its output did not meet the required structure, the system used a deterministic evidence compiler instead. This fallback is significant. It does not attempt a more persuasive answer. It preserves quotations, source identity and limits without adding an unsupported relation. All final documentary claims in the reported cases were checked against their retrieved chunk and quotation provenance.

Twelve cases were designed as stress tests rather than as a representative historical sample. Four ask about known relationships; four about contested interpretations; and four about information the digitised corpus may not establish. Evaluation considered four questions: did retrieval surface relevant traces; did synthesis preserve their different status; did it retain ambiguity; and did it recognise evidential limits? Researcher judgement remained present at each stage, including prompt design, reading of returned records and provenance validation.

The cases were intentionally demanding. A simple named-entity search can find a familiar person in a document. It is less able to distinguish a person named in authority data from a person whose activity is directly described in the passage. Likewise, a model may readily summarise several definitions of a concept but be tempted to call them a consensus or disagreement. The missingness questions were designed to test the strongest temptation of all: to answer a historically important question because it has been asked, even when the relevant decision record, reception evidence or contemporary attribution is not present.

The protocol therefore treated a useful negative result as an outcome, not an error. When a model response was structurally invalid or made an unsupported leap, the system did not ask it to be more imaginative. It retained the evidence through deterministic compilation and identified the limit. Eleven of the twelve cases used this deterministic or explicit no-cross-source-inference behaviour at the final stage. This indicates that the experimental contribution lies in the combined design of retrieval, evidence typing and review, rather than in unrestricted generative interpretation.

The experiment does not test whether a small model can reconstruct the history of the DDR. It tests whether a bounded retrieval and inference system can support inspectable relationships between archival traces.

## 5. Findings

The results are neither a simple success nor a simple failure. All twelve cases returned relevant traces, and every final documentary claim passed chunk-and-quotation provenance validation. Yet the most valuable behaviour was often restraint rather than synthesis. The experiment worked best when it made a narrow connection clear while leaving larger historical questions open.

### 5.1 Relationships surfaced without completing a role

The first four cases show how a system can surface useful connections while avoiding an inflated account. In the Job 171 case, retrieval brought forward five passages from April 1977 records describing interface-design activity, human-factors concerns, man-computer dialogue criteria and CEDAR Mark 2. Separate authority information identified the project title, its Science Research Council funding and Pierre Goumain as project lead. Keeping these layers separate matters: the records connect the project to activities and outputs, but the quoted passages do not establish Goumain's individual causal role in each activity.

The Bruce Archer case similarly returned a 1975 Frank Height memo that directly refers to Archer's lectures and tutorials, alongside course and lecture material. The result supports a limited statement about teaching in the recorded arrangement. It does not establish a complete pedagogy, supervision practice or student experience. In the John Wood case, two near-duplicate course listings identify a lecture and a computer-console case study, while a separate brochure credits Wood and Douglas Tomkin with a police command-and-control project. The duplication is not independent confirmation, and the records do not show that Wood alone designed the consoles.

The Design Education Unit case makes the same point at the level of metadata. A parent archive record uses controlled keywords for both Ken Baynes and Phil Roberts; a separate publication page jointly credits them for *Design as a medium for learning*. These are meaningful archival connections. They do not prove collaboration, shared responsibility or a complete account of either person's role. The useful result is not a definitive network of people, but an inspectable route between a catalogue assertion, named records and a direct joint attribution.

This distinction between relation and role has practical value for archive users. A reader pursuing a question about Goumain, Archer, Wood, Baynes or Roberts is not left with an empty result merely because the corpus cannot support a full biographical claim. Instead, the system provides a starting point: the relevant project or course record, the available passage, the archival description, and an explicit account of what still requires archival research. It supports movement from a question to documents without silently converting that movement into historical proof.

### 5.2 Different formulations are not necessarily disagreement

The next four cases concerned concepts that are easy to smooth into a single institutional view. The question of how the DDR understood design research returned an Archer course outline, George Mallen's account, an inter-university programme, and a Job 171 passage. Together they show recurring language of enquiry, discipline and methods. They do not establish one settled DDR definition. The deterministic fallback was activated in this case, preserving four direct formulations rather than making a cross-source inference.

A comparison of design, science and research produced two Archer formulations and an RCA calendar statement of departmental activity. Archer's texts present a designerly mode of enquiry as comparable with, but distinct from, scientific and scholarly modes. The calendar states an institutional remit involving contract, academic and student research. These are related but not identical statements. The system correctly avoided assigning every contributor a single view or calling the variation a dispute.

The temporal comparison of the Design Education Unit was especially revealing. It returned a pre-closure Rector's report, a 1984 prospectus, Baynes's 1984 letter and proposal, and Christopher Frayling's 2013 oral-history interview. The later interview may be important testimony, but it cannot by itself establish the Unit's origin, named roles or facts about the DDR as if it were a contemporary institutional record. Keeping date and source form visible prevents retrospective interpretation from quietly overriding the historical boundary.

The systematic-design-process case produced different emphases: cognitive limits on exhaustive search, school design process, and Archer's account of systematic analysis alongside creative action. The evidence supports the claim that several formulations survive. It does not establish direct debate, competing schools or a single conceptual history. Here, not calling difference a disagreement is an analytical achievement.

This restraint is not a refusal to interpret. It is an interpretation of the record's evidential condition. The system identifies a family of formulations and makes comparison possible, but declines to claim more than the materials show. A researcher may subsequently investigate whether correspondence, publications, meeting records or un-digitised files reveal a debate. The interface should make that next step imaginable. In this sense, the generated response is useful when it becomes a well-specified research question rather than a final answer.

### 5.3 Scoped missingness makes a limit available for discussion

The final four cases tested whether the system could name an absence without turning it into a claim about the past. The closure question returned a December 1985 Rector's report with a projected August 1986 closure, and a 2013 Frayling interview containing tentative recollection. Neither record establishes who decided to close the DDR, why the decision was made, or the policy and funding rationale. The projected date also conflicts with the experiment's June 1985 closure boundary. The result therefore records a temporal and documentary limit rather than producing a reason for closure.

The computing case returned a 1972 report on an institutional CAD-centre arrangement and Mallen's later statement that, as far as he knew, the initiative was almost entirely Patrick Purcell's. This is valuable evidence of a qualified attribution and of an institutional arrangement. It is not a contemporaneous decision record or proof of a single initiator.

The Design in General Education case distinguishes programme intention from reception. A programme sets out aims for teachers and advisers; Frayling later describes curriculum impact and contributors' response. The surviving digitised record does not establish how teachers or pupils received the work, how widely it was adopted, or whether intended users shared the programme's view. Similarly, records concerning Henrietta Ryott establish a staff-list presence, manuscript-typing credit and a project-secretary credit, while an employment authority provides dates and job title as context. They do not establish her full duties, decision-making authority or research contribution.

Across all twelve cases, the experiment ends with an explicit closure: what this digitised corpus does not establish. This is *scoped missingness*. It is not a failure notice and it is not proof that the missing thing never existed. It is a statement about a defined body of accessible material, a retrieval route and a historical question.

The phrase "does not establish" is deliberately stronger than a generic warning that AI may be inaccurate, and weaker than a claim of historical absence. It ties uncertainty to a visible scope. In the closure case, for example, the scope contains a projected closure statement and a later tentative account, but no decision record or contemporaneous rationale. The appropriate outcome is not an invented explanation, nor an assertion that no explanation survives anywhere. It is a statement of what a user can and cannot responsibly conclude from this evidence surface.

### 5.4 What the system adds, and what it must not add

The experiment's central result is a change in the shape of an answer. A conventional search result can return a list of documents. An unconstrained chatbot can turn the same material into a fluent paragraph. The system tested here attempts something in between: it presents a bounded account that remains connected to records and makes its limits explicit. That account is valuable only if users can reopen it. The generated prose is not the final destination; it is a route back into the archive.

This also explains why an apparently less capable response can be the more responsible one. In several concept and comparison cases, the final generative stage did not meet the required evidence structure. The deterministic fallback did not repair the answer by inventing connective language. It preserved source-specific claims and withheld the relation. A system judged only by conversational smoothness might treat this as failure. For archival research, it is a useful form of discipline. It prevents a technical limitation from becoming a historical assertion.

The same applies to retrieval. Returning relevant passages is not enough if the selection makes a narrow source appear representative of an institution, or if near-duplicate documents create a false impression of corroboration. The Wood case demonstrates this clearly: two similar course listings support one teaching attribution, not two independent confirmations. The experiment therefore treats documentary diversity, source form and record identity as part of the answer's meaning. An answer built from several different records is not automatically stronger; it needs to show why the records are distinct and what each contributes.

The results matrix should consequently be read as an audit trail, not as a leaderboard. It records where retrieval improved, where a fallback was needed, what provenance checks passed, and what claims were held back. This mode of evaluation is appropriate to a heritage setting because the question is not only whether an answer resembles an expected response. It is whether the route to that answer has remained open to challenge.

## 6. Discussion: archival activation and scoped missingness

The experiment suggests that archival activation does not fill an archive's gaps. It reorganises the conditions under which gaps and their surrounding traces can be encountered. In this case, AI was most helpful not when it sounded certain, but when the interface and workflow made certainty difficult to fake.

This is a different account of value from a system designed chiefly to provide a fast answer. The system can make a dispersed record easier to approach: it gathers a course listing, a project report, an archive description and a later interview around a question. But it must also leave the differences between them in view. A researcher or public user should be able to ask: is this a quotation, a catalogue connection, a retrospective statement, or a synthesis? What is the date? What record is absent? Which part is an interpretation?

This has implications for synthetic heritage. Generated text can support access and reflection, but it should not be treated as a neutral layer laid over the archive. It actively selects, joins and names. The ethical and scholarly question is whether those operations can be inspected and contested. A system that shows its source passages, keeps authority metadata separate, and states limits does not remove interpretation. It makes interpretation more accountable.

Five practical principles emerge. First, preserve provenance at passage level so that a claim can be reopened in its source context. Second, mark synthesis: direct evidence, archival metadata and generated interpretation should not look alike. Third, retain uncertainty through scoped missingness rather than offering a polished completion. Fourth, keep temporal and source-family distinctions active, especially where later testimony speaks about earlier events. Fifth, keep researcher judgement visible. The system may retrieve and arrange; it cannot decide that a connection is historically sufficient.

These principles also make room for disagreement. In a cultural-heritage setting, the aim should not be to make every archive speak with one voice. It should be to allow different forms of evidence, institutional descriptions and lived recollections to remain available without forcing them into agreement. The potential contribution of generative AI is therefore not only speed or scale. It may be a way to make the work of careful comparison more practicable, provided that the system is designed to expose rather than conceal its limits.

There is also a public-facing reason for this approach. Interfaces often give generated text a visual authority that exceeds its basis: a neat paragraph can appear more decisive than a difficult scan, a long catalogue entry or an uncertain interview. The UAT workflow used here counters that tendency by keeping evidence tracing and missingness work alongside synthesis. This does not guarantee careful use. It gives a user concrete opportunities to check a statement before repeating it, and it keeps the interface from implying that the system has located a definitive answer whenever it has located a relevant phrase.

The approach may be especially useful where cultural memory is contested or institutionally uneven. It does not solve questions of representation, jurisdiction, access or harm that arise in many heritage contexts, and the DDR case cannot stand in for those contexts. But it offers a transferable discipline: before a synthetic account is treated as knowledge, ask what it is built from, who or what is absent, what kind of source is speaking, and which judgement remains human.

For institutions, this implies that provenance is not a supplementary technical feature. It is part of the public meaning of an archival system. A record identifier, page reference, date and source label may look like administrative detail, yet together they let a reader distinguish a report from a recollection, a direct quotation from an inference, and a gap in the digitised corpus from a claim about historical non-existence. Designing these distinctions into an interface is a form of interpretative care.

The approach does not demand that every user become an archivist or technical specialist. Its aim is the opposite: to make the basis of an answer understandable in ordinary language. Labels such as "direct document", "archive description", "later interview" and "not established by this corpus" can be meaningful without exposing users to the full machinery of the system. The important point is that the simplification remains honest. A readable interface should reduce unnecessary difficulty, not remove the uncertainty that gives a record its proper historical status.

There is a productive tension here. Archives are often approached because they promise discovery, while a provenance-led system repeatedly says that certain things cannot yet be known. This need not make the system unhelpful. A careful limit can direct attention towards the next research action: consult an un-digitised series, compare a later recollection with contemporary correspondence, request a fuller file, or ask a new question of the catalogue. The system's refusal is therefore not a dead end. It is an invitation to continue research without pretending that research has already finished.

## 7. Limitations

This is an exploratory experiment. It reports twelve designed cases within one archive, one retrieval design and one small local model configuration. The findings are not statistically generalisable, and they should not be read as proof that the same routes, prompts or safeguards will work in another institutional setting. The digitised evidence surface is itself partial: some materials remain unavailable, image-only, unextracted or insufficiently described.

The evaluation also depends on researcher judgement. A researcher selected the questions, defined the retrieval routes, assessed the returned traces and checked final provenance. That judgement is not eliminated by automation and should not be hidden behind it. Future work should test the protocol across additional archives, model families and researchers, including user studies of whether visible provenance and scoped missingness actually improve critical reading.

These are not incidental weaknesses. They define the conditions under which the experiment's claims are valid. The system is a research instrument for working with a specified evidence surface, not a general historical authority.

## 8. Conclusion

This paper asked whether retrieval-augmented inference could activate testamentary traces in an incomplete design archive without claiming to recover a complete past. The experiment indicates that it can contribute when its purpose is carefully limited. Relevant records can be surfaced across dispersed document types, but the resulting account remains trustworthy only when its evidential path stays open to inspection.

The proposed protocol is straightforward: preserve provenance; distinguish direct evidence from synthesis; retain uncertainty through scoped missingness; test the process with more than one researcher, archive and model; and keep human judgement visible. These are not additions to an otherwise autonomous system. They are the conditions of its responsible use.

Generative AI is most useful to archival research when it does not claim to complete the past. Its contribution is to make the surviving record, the interpretations made from it, and the limits around both more available for critical examination.
